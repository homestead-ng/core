Homestead-NG

Working concept, for discussion. This is a rough starting point for exploring a self-hosted, shared workspace for the systems that keep a household running: money, purchasing, supplies, meals, maintenance, schedules, and important records. It is intentionally broad and incomplete. Nothing here is a final product scope, schedule, or implementation decision.

Ideas to explore

  • Shared household picture: Household members can work from common information rather than disconnected personal copies.
  • Connected responsibilities: Information in one area could help with another, such as a meal plan identifying supplies to buy.
  • Deliberate spending: Non-routine purchases could have a thoughtful request and approval path, while ordinary restocking stays easy.
  • Household ownership: Self-hosting and understandable data handling are early product values to examine.
  • Free and open source: The software costs nothing to use; no license fee, required subscription, paid account, or paywalled core feature. Donations and sponsorships are welcome. Optional hosted service or paid support may be considered later, without limiting the self-hosted software. Third-party bank services and infrastructure may charge separately.
  • Useful at home and away: The Go backend and WebUI in this repository are the primary application. A native Android companion is a later client for quick interactions on the move.

Possible areas

These are candidate areas, not a settled module list. Some may be out of scope, combined, or reordered as the product becomes clearer.

  1. Finance and budgeting: Give income and account balances purposes, plan for irregular expenses, and understand committed as well as already-spent funds. Optional shared and personal cash wallets can be tracked alongside bank accounts.
  2. Purchasing and approvals: Capture a purchase idea, apply household rules, request another member's decision when appropriate, and organize approved purchases for a shopping trip.
  3. Inventory and pantry: Track useful supplies by storage area, surface low stock, and help members use perishable items in time.
  4. Meals and recipes: Plan meals from shared recipes, compare ingredient needs with available stock, and account for preparation.
  5. Assets and maintenance: Keep household and vehicle information, service history, and recurring maintenance work together.
  6. Tasks, projects, and calendar: Coordinate chores, household projects, appointments, and recurring routines across members.
  7. Records and continuity: Organize important documents, care information, and emergency procedures with appropriate privacy.
  8. People and relationships: Organize contacts, family and friend relationships, birthdays, gift ideas, visits, and optional reminders without making a contact a household account.

Example interactions

Meal planning and restocking

A member plans meals for the week. The household compares ingredient needs with pantry stock, creates a shopping list for shortages, and updates budget and inventory when purchases are recorded.

Purchase request and follow-through

A member requests a non-routine item against a planned spending category. A household rule determines whether it can proceed or needs another member's review. An approved request reserves room in the plan until the purchase is recorded or the request is closed.

Maintenance and replenishment

A scheduled maintenance task is completed and recorded against a household asset. Parts used could reduce tracked stock and prompt a restocking task when supplies run low.

Modular platform direction

The emerging design is a household platform with independently deployable modules. Core responsibilities under consideration include identity, household access, module discovery and shared infrastructure. Budgeting is a module, not a requirement for every installation: a future recipe-only household should not need to install it. Modules may declare required or optional dependencies and request narrowly scoped permissions. Exact APIs, data ownership, compatibility, and cross-module financial consistency still need contracts.

The initial prototype is intended to publish Docker images and document networking, storage, and interconnection; operators manage their own Compose files or containers. A future managed mode could install modules when Core is explicitly granted Docker access, including in an optional VM appliance. Docker socket access grants powerful host-level authority and must not be given to modules. Managed installation, independent module repositories, a module template, signed catalogs, and external repositories remain to be designed.

Core is envisioned as the home for identity, household access, module discovery, and shared infrastructure. Budgeting, procurement, recipes, and other capabilities could be separately distributed services with their own data. A module manifest could declare compatible versions, required or optional dependencies, and requested permissions. A recipe-only household should not need a financial module. Whether procurement requires budgeting is open.

Proposed integration boundaries include versioned APIs, explicit permissions, and clear data ownership rather than shared database access. Financial changes need atomic ownership within their responsible service and defined recovery for cross-service failures. Installing, upgrading, disabling, and removing modules must be designed with dependencies, data preservation, backups, and offline use in mind. These details are not yet approved contracts.

For distribution, explore trusted first-party and external module repositories with signed metadata and verified artifacts. PrintMaster's current update-trust work uses TUF and is a reference to investigate, not an implementation already adopted here. A signature establishes artifact trust under its key assumptions; it does not make third-party code safe. Modules must not receive Docker-socket or management credentials; their APIs still require authentication and authorization. A separately managed VM or explicit Core socket access would be an operator choice, not a prerequisite for the initial container deployment.

Core is hosted at homestead-ng/core; separate repositories for individual modules are intended. A module template and shared API/SDK are possible starting points. The preferred domain is homestead-ng.org; registration is not confirmed here. The initial first-party modules are intended to be free. Future paid add-ons and activation support are ideas for discussion, not approved changes to the earlier free-to-use software direction. Optional hosting and support are separate ideas. Any pricing, licensing, or activation proposal needs an explicit owner decision.

People and relationships

An optional People and Relationships module could help households remember and coordinate without treating relationships as sales leads. Possible features include shared contacts and relationship context; birthdays and anniversaries; gift ideas, preferences, and visit notes; and optional reminders to reach out or plan a get-together. Import and export in standard contact formats could come first, with contact sync considered later.

Optional integrations could link people to Calendar, Recipes, or Procurement, but those modules should not be required for a basic contact list. A contact is not automatically a household user. Whether individual notes can be private, how sharing and deletion work, and how sensitive contact information is protected remain open questions.

Community ideas to explore

A household-management discussion highlighted fragmented calendars, lists, messages, and notes. Ideas raised there include a shared calendar compatible with Google Calendar, read-only calendar sharing, organized shopping lists with favorites and timely updates, task deadlines and delivery status, shared notes with links, household contacts, and recipe-to-grocery workflows. These are potential module ideas, not commitments. Contact and family information would need careful privacy and access decisions.

One possible way to group the work

This is a conversation aid, not a roadmap or commitment about sequence.

  1. Explore a small finance and purchasing workflow, including household review, committed-spend visibility, and basic shopping lists.
  2. Explore receipt capture and basic inventory, including human review of unfamiliar store descriptions.
  3. Explore recipe, meal, and supply planning based on what is already on hand.
  4. Consider maintenance, tasks, projects, calendars, and sensitive family records after their needs and boundaries are better understood.

Questions for refinement

  • Which household problem is worth solving first, and for whom?
  • What is the smallest useful first version, and what should it explicitly leave out?
  • How should household membership, roles, permissions, and personal privacy work?
  • What do budgeting, committed spending, transaction handling, and reconciliation mean to the household?
  • What data must be exportable, how should backups work, and how should sensitive records be protected?
  • What should mobile use, offline access, notifications, and external calendar connections feel like?

Current repository boundary

Owner direction, 2026-11-19: This repository is for the Go backend and primary WebUI. The Android companion will live in a separate repository after the API contract is sufficiently defined and a usable main-app prototype is available to develop against. This establishes client and repo sequencing; it does not approve the domain scope or feature behavior.

Owner direction, 2026-11-24: One self-hosted server serves one household; multi-tenancy is not planned. The prototype WebUI is desktop-first; the Android companion remains a separate later client.

Owner direction, 2026-11-19: The first prototype should explore a two-adult household using USD, envelopes and allocation, purchase requests, approval or rejection by the other member, and reserved funds. Google OIDC is the chosen sign-in direction. SimpleFIN Bridge plus CSV/OFX import are the initial bank-data direction; Plaid is a later option. Imported transactions can be split across envelopes, with suggested purchase-request matches requiring member confirmation.

Details remain open: data model, exact OIDC/session flow, account connection setup, request lifecycle edge cases, transaction corrections, and API routes and payloads. The draft CORE contract records owner choices and candidate behavior for review; its proposed requirements are not blanket approval for implementation.
